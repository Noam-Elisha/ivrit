The shape of the six months

Everything from your four spreadsheets — 503 words in total: 134 verbs, 254 nouns, 83 adjectives and 33 connectors — is laid out across 26 weeks. Nothing has been left out.

Weeks 1–21 (Phase 1) cover all 503 words. Verbs appear in the infinitive (the root form) and the present tense only, exactly as you asked — the past tense is deliberately held back so you're not juggling two tenses while the vocabulary is still new.

Weeks 22–26 (Phase 2) take the past tense on directly, taught by pattern rather than word by word. Once you can hear that kotev → katavti and soger → sagarti are the same move, all 134 verbs open up at once. Review of the Phase 1 vocabulary continues right through, so nothing goes cold.

A week

  • Days 1–5 — five new words, grouped by theme, plus tiered review. 15–20 minutes.
  • Day 6 — a review of the week's 25 words, in quiz mode. 10 minutes.
  • Day 7 — off. Or five minutes on whatever felt shaky.

How the review is spaced

Each study day brings back five earlier sets at expanding intervals: roughly 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks and 2 months after you first met them. That's the spacing that research on forgetting keeps landing on — you revisit a word just as it's starting to slip, which is exactly when the repetition does the most work.

In practice that's about 25 review items on top of the 5 new ones. The oldest tier is a quick pass — if the meaning comes back within a second or two, move on. Don't linger.

Using quiz mode

The Hide Hebrew and Hide English buttons in the toolbar blur one side of every card. Tap a card to reveal it. Hiding the English (so you're producing meaning from Hebrew) is easier; hiding the Hebrew (producing Hebrew from English) is harder and worth more — use it on the older review tiers once the words are familiar.

The mnemonics

Every word has an association, and they come in three kinds: sound-alikes (tzarich — you need to be rich), root connections that link a new word to one you already know (sefer book → sofer writer → sifriya library), and cultural anchors (Rosh Hashanah is the head of the year). The root connections are the ones that compound: Hebrew is built from three-letter roots, and every one you recognise makes the next dozen words cheaper to learn.

Notes on the source spreadsheets

A handful of rows had small errors or gaps, which have been corrected here:

  • Hurt mixed two different verbs — lehach'iv and lifgoa. Kept as lifgoa / pogea, the everyday one.
  • Cuddle listed lefanek in the present but the past of lechabek (to hug). Kept as lefanek throughout.
  • Return (something) had the forms for lachzor (to go back). Corrected to lehachzir / machzir.
  • Garden listed the noun gina as its infinitive. Corrected to leganen.
  • Need mixed tzarich with the more formal lehizdakek. Kept as tzarich.
  • Enjoy had the past as heneti; the correct form is nehe'neti.
  • Frustrated was mevutsal; the standard word is metuskal.
  • Most was written hachat; it's hachi (for superlatives) or rov (for "the majority").
  • Window was missing its Hebrew: חַלּוֹן.
  • Several rows had stray Latin letters inside Hebrew words (Bikas-nu, Shin-iti, Hirgash-ti) — retyped cleanly.

Your progress is saved

The app remembers where you were, which days you've marked done, and whether quiz mode was on. Close the tab, come back next week, and it opens exactly where you left off — the Continue button in the sidebar jumps straight to your first unfinished day.

This is stored in the browser itself, not in an account, which means two things: nothing is uploaded anywhere, and progress doesn't follow you between devices. If you'll study on both a phone and a laptop, pick one as the place you tick days off.

Bookmark this page, or add it to your home screen, and it behaves like an app.

If you fall behind

Don't restart and don't try to catch up by doubling. Just carry on from where you stopped — the spaced review will pull the missed words back through on its own within a couple of weeks. Missing days is expected and the schedule is built to absorb it.
